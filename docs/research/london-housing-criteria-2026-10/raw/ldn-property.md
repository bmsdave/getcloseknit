# Stream: London property-specific factors (October 2026)

Scope: property-level criteria that are distinctive in London and that buyers, renters, agents and valuers use to screen and price a home. Covers tenure and lease, service charges, leasehold reform, cladding and building safety, EPC, building type, floor level and lifts, size and £/sqft, ceiling height, outside space, basements, ground risks (London clay, knotweed), conservation areas, and NTSELAT material information.

Evidence quality key: [P] peer-reviewed or academic working paper, [G] government, [J] trade/professional press or consultancy research, [F] forum thread, [W] other web. Most numbers below are from sources fetched and read; where only a search snippet was seen it is flagged "(snippet only)".

---

## 1. Why London is different at the property level

London's housing stock is flat-heavy and leasehold-heavy. Mumsnet posters note that at mid-market budgets in inner London a buyer is effectively forced into a leasehold flat, because freehold houses are out of reach and flats are almost always sold leasehold [F: https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/property/3343093-leasehold-vs-freehold]. This makes a cluster of legal and financial attributes (lease length, ground rent, service charge, freeholder quality, building safety status) into *primary* screening criteria in London, whereas in most other UK cities and in most world cities they are minor or non-existent. Spear's describes leasehold as near-universal in English apartment buildings yet almost unknown elsewhere in the world, which is the core reason overseas and migrant buyers find London flats confusing [J/W: https://spearswms.com/wealth/the-danger-of-leaseholds-in-london/, 2013].

## 2. Primary criterion: tenure and remaining lease length

**Evidence on price effect.** The strongest academic evidence is Giglio, Maggiori and Stroebel (NBER WP 20133; QJE 2015), using the universe of England and Wales transactions with hedonic controls (floor area, flat vs house, other characteristics). Leaseholds with 80-99 years left trade about 16% below otherwise identical freeholds; 100-125 years about 10%; 125-150 years about 8%; 150-300 years about 3%; 700+ years statistically indistinguishable from freehold [P: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20133/w20133.pdf]. Two points matter for a London tool: (a) the authors report that discounts outside London are very similar to London, so the lease-length curve is a national rule, but its *weight* is greater in London because more of the stock is leasehold flats; (b) they found market participants' priors (buyers, valuers, agents) were very dispersed, and some valuers thought discounts were smaller than the data show. That is a direct sign that lease length is under-modelled in practice.

**The 80-year cliff.** Below 80 years, statutory lease-extension premiums include "marriage value", so costs jump. Which? gives an example of roughly £4,000-£6,000 to extend with 80+ years left versus nearly £50,000 once under 80, and notes 8% of residential leases have 80-90 years remaining [W: https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/should-you-wait-for-cheaper-lease-extensions-aL84a6p9rINn, Aug 2026]. On Mumsnet (Sept 2026) a buyer considering a sub-50-year one-bed in Bloomsbury at about £425k was told that a quoted £40k extension probably excluded marriage value and that a realistic figure could be £90k+ plus fees, and that mortgage finance on very short leases is hard [F: https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/property/5583373-buying-a-flat-with-a-short-lease-and-extending-it]. Knight Frank's leasehold partner Vanessa Griffiths stresses lease length drives both marketability and mortgageability; leases in the 82-85 year band are at risk of slipping under 80 during reform uncertainty [J: https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/better-business/2026/09/09/extend-now-or-wait-the-short-lease-dilemma-under-leasehold-reform-griffiths/].

**Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 (LFRA) status as of Oct 2026.** The Act will remove marriage value, cap the ground rent used in valuation at 0.1% of freehold vacant-possession value, and allow 990-year extensions at a peppercorn rent. It cannot operate until the Secretary of State prescribes deferment and capitalisation rates. The government consultation on those rates was published 7 Sept 2026 and closes 21 Oct 2026. Options include keeping Sportelli rates (5% flats, 4.75% houses), updating them (GAD analysis gave about 6.3% flats and 6.05% houses as of Sept 2025, with alternatives of 5.05-5.85%), or other approaches; capitalisation rates in tribunal decisions cluster at 6-7% (88% of cases). Worked example: a £250k flat with 100 years left and escalating ground rent costs about £4,576 to extend under the proposed method [G: https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/leasehold-enfranchisement-valuation-rates/leasehold-enfranchisement-valuation-rates]. Griffiths shows the sensitivity: for a £250k flat with 80 years left, moving the deferment rate from 5% to 4% roughly doubles the reversion element (about £5.0k to £10.8k) [J: mortgagesolutions link above]. Which? states the earliest implementation is 2027 [W: Which? link above].

*Disagreement:* Which? (Aug 2026) reports a 23 Sept 2026 closing date; the gov.uk consultation page shows it closing on 21 Oct 2026, consistent with an extension. Use the gov.uk date. A higher prescribed deferment rate (6%+) would make extensions cheaper for leaseholders; a lower one would raise costs, so "wait for reform" is not a guaranteed saving.

**Share of freehold.** Forum and agent views agree it adds control (residents appoint managing agents, no external ground rent) but only a modest value uplift. Mumsnet posters note well-run leasehold blocks can beat badly run share-of-freehold ones and that SoF requires cooperative neighbours [F: https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/property/3343093-leasehold-vs-freehold]. A search snippet attributes a £14.5k-£27k uplift range to SoF depending on lease length (snippet only, source not verified).

**How agents and portals encode it.** Under NTSELAT Part A, tenure must appear in every listing; for leasehold, remaining lease term, ground rent and service charge are expected up front [J: https://propertyindustryeye.com/?p=136304; J: https://www.rics.org/news-insights/material-information-what-to-know-and-what-happens-next].

## 3. Primary criterion (flats): service charge and ground rent

**Level and trend.** Hamptons (Mar 2026): average London service charge £2,801 per year (about £233 per month) vs £2,405 nationally; up 41.2% over five years in London (32.6% nationally) and 64.5% over a decade (55.6% nationally). Hamptons attributes London's higher level to taller buildings with more amenities. 37% of flats now have a service charge above 1% of their value (29% five years earlier), and lenders have tightened underwriting to exclude flats where charges routinely exceed 1% of value [J: https://www.landlordtoday.co.uk/breaking-news/2026/03/soaring-service-charges-on-leasehold-flats-hit-capital-values/].

Hamptons (Feb 2025) priced amenities into the annual charge: a gym adds about £561, a lift about £364, a concierge about £892; more than half of leaseholders pay more in service charge than in council tax; buyers and lenders are increasingly wary of disproportionate charges [J: https://thenegotiator.co.uk/news/rocketing-leasehold-service-charges-putting-off-buyers-says-big-estate-agency/].

**Practical screening rule emerging from the evidence:** service charge as a % of value (flag above 1%), service charge per sqft, and whether it buys amenities the buyer will use. A lift, concierge and gym raise the charge; a walk-up conversion has a low charge but a floor-level penalty (section 6).

**Forum signal.** A Feb 2025 MSE thread about two £350k one-bed flats in a London housing-association block that would not sell after six months of price cuts: replies cited undisclosed service charges, pay-as-you-go district heating, a main-road aspect, commercial units on the ground floor (lender aversion), shared-ownership resale stigma, cladding history even with an EWS1, and competing new-build stock nearby [F: https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6588275/why-aren-t-properties-in-this-block-selling]. This is a good list of secondary "red flag" filters London buyers apply to flats.

**Historic abuse narrative.** Spear's catalogued London cases of inflated service charges and insurance mark-ups (e.g. a 33% insurance mark-up example) and costly enfranchisement litigation [J/W: https://spearswms.com/wealth/the-danger-of-leaseholds-in-london/, 2013]. Mumsnet posters repeat the worry about unexpected major-works bills and fees for freeholder consent to alterations [F: mumsnet leasehold vs freehold link].

## 4. Primary deal-breaker (blocks): cladding, EWS1 and the Building Safety Act

- Building Safety Act 2022 protections (in force 28 June 2022) cover qualifying leaseholders in buildings of 11m+ or 5+ storeys: no cost for cladding remediation, and capped costs for non-cladding defects; leaseholders in homes worth under £325k in London (£175k elsewhere) pay nothing for non-cladding works [G: https://www.gov.uk/government/news/leaseholders-protected-from-unfair-cladding-costs-as-governments-building-safety-reforms-come-into-force]. A search snippet adds the caps of £15k London and £10k elsewhere (snippet only, consistent with law).
- In practice cladding remains a marketability issue even when an EWS1 exists. The MSE thread above shows buyers and lenders still hesitate where a block had cladding [F: MSE link above].
- RICS warns agents completing Part C building-safety fields are mostly not qualified to advise on building-safety cost exposure, and that material information is no substitute for a survey [J: RICS link above].
- London-specific: the higher non-cladding cap (£15k) and higher value threshold (£325k) are explicitly London-only parameters in law, which a London tool should encode separately from the rest of England.

## 5. EPC rating and minimum energy standards

- **Sales market effect is modest.** Nationwide (2021) found A/B homes about 1.7% dearer than D, F/G about 3.5% cheaper, and C/E near zero [J: https://www.propertyreporter.co.uk/property/epc-ratings-having-limited-impact-on-house-prices-nationwide.html]. A 2024 Nationwide update (snippet only) reported +1.6% for A/B, -1.4% for F/G, and about +12% for A/B in buy-to-let. Academic work by Fuerst, McAllister, Nanda and Wyatt (Energy Economics 2015) found a positive EPC-price per sqm relationship across about 400k English sales, larger for flats and terraces (snippet only; repository blocked).
- **Rental market effect is larger and regulatory.** Government response of 21 Jan 2026: privately rented homes in England and Wales must reach EPC C by 1 Oct 2030, under a new multi-metric EPC (fabric performance mandatory, plus heating-system efficiency or smart readiness), with a £10,000 cost cap, 10-year exemptions, and fines up to £30,000 [W: https://www.elmhurstenergy.co.uk/blog/2026/01/22/domestic-private-rented-properties-required-to-meet-epc-band-c-by-october-2030/].
- **London nuance:** London has many solid-wall Victorian/Edwardian conversions and listed or conservation-area homes where upgrades are restricted (no double glazing in listed buildings; see section 8), so EPC upgrade cost and feasibility matter more for London landlords than the sales premium suggests. This is an inference from combining the MEES rules with the period-property forum evidence.

## 6. Building type, floor level, lifts and ceiling height

**Period conversion vs purpose-built vs new-build.**
- PeriodProperty forum (Grade II Georgian conversion, central London): sound travels through timber floors, listed status blocks ceiling soundproofing and double glazing, high ceilings but awkward layouts, costly restoration of shutters and cornices; one poster concluded conversions give a lower standard of living than purpose-built flats, another described noise problems in a Highgate Victorian conversion [F: https://periodproperty.co.uk/forum/threads/if-youre-debating-whether-to-buy-a-flat-in-a-georgian-house.14203].
- New-build premium: LCP (2016) found new-build flats in prime central London priced about 20% above resale (£1,701 vs £1,418 per sqft) and about 25% higher in nine central boroughs, with the premium eroding on resale and new-build areas growing more slowly (7.3% vs 9.3% a year since 2003) [J: https://2025.financialreporter.co.uk/finance-news/london-new-build-schemes-charge-20-premium.html]. The MSE thread shows resale flats in large new or HA blocks compete mainly on price against new stock [F: MSE link].

**Floor level and lifts.**
- A London agent advised pricing a fourth-floor walk-up roughly 20% below an equivalent first-floor flat; the buyer pool narrows to younger buyers, though the absence of a lift lowers the service charge and there are no neighbours above [W: https://www.cityam.com/sell-7/].
- Ground-floor flats trade off accessibility and garden access against noise from the entrance and security concerns (search snippet of the same City AM material).
- In stucco and Georgian buildings the raised-ground and first floors (piano nobile) have the tallest ceilings and usually command a premium [W: https://www.thejc.com/sponsored/ceiling-heights-must-stack-up-for-home-hunters-qkblcuh1, Feb 2024, sponsored content].
- Towers: a search snippet referenced a RICS-catalogued study on a London tower height premium of about 1.5% per floor and 1-3% of price on average (snippet only). Knight Frank penthouse research reported penthouses about 35% dearer per sqft across five global cities and about 43% in London (snippet only; KF page returned 404).

**Ceiling height.** Arlington Residential estimates about 15% extra value where ceilings are well above the norm; the UK norm is about 2.4m, 2.7m+ is sought, 3.7m is exceptional; European buyers (used to Paris, Rome, Vienna stock) are especially sensitive to volume [W: JC link above]. Dexters notes double-height spaces are rare and command a premium in prime central London but gives no figure [W: https://www.dexters.co.uk/dexters-blog/15742-sky-high].

## 7. Size and £/sqft

- London agents and valuers routinely price on £/sqft, while the wider UK market historically relies on bedroom counts (search snippets: Zoopla, HOA). RICS measurement standards are not legally required for agents, and floor-plan areas are a known source of inconsistency (snippet only).
- Example dispersion: a 2021 analysis put London flats at about £956/sqft, terraces about £891/sqft, semis about £985, detached about £1,113 [W: https://propertyreporter.co.uk/property/which-property-type-offers-the-best-value-for-money-for-london-homebuyers.html]. The average sizes in that source look implausibly small for detached houses (883 sqft), so treat it as illustrative only.
- Part B of NTSELAT material information requires the number, type and measurements of rooms in listings, which pushes portals towards consistent size data [J: PIE and RICS links].
- The leasehold discount study controls for floor area, so lease length effects are additional to size [P: NBER link].

## 8. Outside space, basements, conservation areas and listed buildings

**Outside space.** Marsh & Parsons (2013) estimated Londoners pay about 20% more for homes with gardens, terraces or park proximity; examples include a Bayswater one-bed with patio at +16% (£75k) and a Kensington flat overlooking Kensington Gardens at +36% [J: https://www.propertyreporter.co.uk/property/londoners-pay-20-premium-for-outdoor-space.html]. Later Marsh & Parsons figures (snippet only) put a balcony or roof terrace at about +12% on average (up to +25% in Chelsea) and communal gardens at +11% (up to +20% in Little Venice and Holland Park); a 2022 snippet put private-garden flats at +19% on asking price. Garden-square access is a distinctive prime-London attribute (PrimeResi, paywalled, not read).

**Basements.** Savills (via City AM, 2016) estimated a basement dig-out could add 10-15% to a £1.5m-£2m London family house; Lurot Brand notes basement space sells for less per sqft than above-ground space because of light and ventilation. RBKC caps basements at 50% of garden area and Westminster charges an £8,000 application fee; double-basement applications doubled in five years to 2015 [W: https://www.cityam.com/economics-iceberg-homes-mega-basements-just-sunk-cost/]. Higher uplift claims (35-96% by borough, from a remortgage-broker analysis; snippet only) conflict with the Savills range and look like gross additions to floor area rather than like-for-like premiums.

**Conservation areas.** LSE research for English Heritage (Ahlfeldt, Holman, Wendland 2012; over 1m Nationwide transactions 1995-2010, 8,000+ conservation areas) found a raw 23% premium falling to about 9% after controls, larger near the centre of an area, spilling 500-700m beyond the boundary, and about 4 points lower in areas "at risk" (snippet only; LSE repository has no full text). The companion paper "Game of Zones" (Ahlfeldt, Moeller, Waights, Wendland; SERC DP 0143) finds that new designations at the margin show no significant price capitalisation, because amenity benefits are offset by restrictions on alteration [P: https://ideas.repec.org/p/ehl/lserod/58349.html]. Interpretation: being inside an established conservation area correlates with a premium, but designation itself does not create one at the margin. Listed status limits upgrades (glazing, soundproofing), affecting EPC and comfort [F: PeriodProperty link].

## 9. Ground risks: London clay subsidence and Japanese knotweed

**Subsidence.** London clay is highly shrinkable. Following the heatwaves of summer 2026, insurers reported record subsidence claims in Q2 2026 averaging about £20,000 per claim (up 15% year on year), with payouts up about 90% over four years to £297m; BGS projects 1.8m UK properties highly susceptible by 2070 (4.2m under high emissions); Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea are named among the most affected boroughs; underpinning costs £20k-£100k+ [W: https://cleantechnica.com/2026/08/25/homes-in-london-are-cracking-as-the-clay-they-are-built-on-shrinks/]. The "up to 25% value reduction" claim in that article is unattributed.
Forums: Mumsnet (2024) posters say underpinning is common among London Victorian/Edwardian homes on clay and so less alarming there; an underpinned house can be more stable; but insurers can be harder, excesses of about £2,500 are typical, and buyers should obtain the structural adequacy certificate and know the cause (trees vs drains) [F: https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/property/5154535-underpinning]. MSE (2013): a chartered surveyor says professionally remediated, warrantied underpinning is not inherently risky and the problem is stigma; insurance transferability and excess can affect lender approval; one poster cites three subsidence claims on one South London house in 20 years [F: https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/4695349/buying-a-house-with-past-subsidence-problems].

**Japanese knotweed.** RICS guidance in force since 23 Mar 2022 dropped the 7m proximity rule for a professional-judgement, management-category approach; research shows little risk to robust buildings, but it can affect amenity; previously knotweed could cut value by up to 15% and block mortgages [J: https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/mortgage-news/2022/02/11/rics-updates-guidance-on-japanese-knotweed/]. Knotweed remains a disclosure item on the TA6 form and is a secondary, not primary, factor.

## 10. Material information (NTSELAT Parts A, B, C)

- Part A (from 2022): council tax band, asking price/rent, tenure (and deposit for lettings).
- Part B (Nov 2023): physical characteristics, number, type and measurements of rooms, utilities (electricity, water, sewerage, heating), broadband, mobile signal, parking.
- Part C (Nov 2023, only where relevant): building safety, restrictions and rights (covenants, rights of way, listing, conservation area), flood and erosion risk, planning permission and nearby development proposals, accessibility and adaptations, coalfield/mining area.
Sources: [J: https://propertyindustryeye.com/?p=136304, Apr 2024]; [J: https://www.rics.org/news-insights/material-information-what-to-know-and-what-happens-next, Nov 2023]; [W: https://inventorybase.co.uk/blog/material-information-property-listings-parts-a-b-c/]. NTSELAT's investigator argues more up-front information saves wasted viewings and later disputes; RICS cautions agents are not surveyors and that portals needed about 12 months to add fields. The NTSELAT landing page notes MHCLG announced (June 2026) an intention to introduce homebuying sales packs, which would move more of this data to before an offer.

Practical implication: Parts A-C are effectively the official checklist of property-level criteria in the UK; London listings are more often affected by the leasehold, building-safety, conservation-area/listing and parking-restriction items than listings elsewhere.

## 11. Primary vs secondary criteria (London property level)

Primary (screening or deal-breakers):
1. Tenure and remaining lease length (80-year threshold; mortgageability).
2. Service charge level and trend (flag >1% of value; amenities driving cost).
3. Building safety status for blocks 11m+ (cladding, EWS1 where asked, BSA qualifying status).
4. Internal area / £ per sqft (London prices on sqft more than other UK cities).
5. Outside space (private garden, balcony, terrace, communal garden).
6. Floor level and lift access (walk-up penalty; piano nobile premium in period stock).

Secondary (price adjusters and due-diligence items):
- Ground rent and its review terms (LFRA caps its weight in valuation).
- Share of freehold vs leasehold (control more than value).
- Building type: period conversion (character, ceilings, noise) vs purpose-built vs new-build (premium that erodes on resale).
- Ceiling height (about 15% for exceptional height in prime).
- EPC (small sales effect, big rental compliance effect from 2030).
- Conservation area / listed status (premium by location, restrictions on works).
- Basement or extension potential (borough policy limits).
- Subsidence history on London clay; Japanese knotweed.
- District heating, commercial units below, main-road aspect, shared-ownership history (forum red flags).

## 12. Comparison notes (beyond London)
- The leasehold discount curve is similar outside London (GMS), but leasehold flats are a much larger share of London transactions.
- Service charges rose faster in the North East (+60.9% over five years) than in London, driven by city-centre amenity schemes, so the "service charge" criterion is spreading to other UK cities (Hamptons via The Negotiator).
- BSA caps and value thresholds are explicitly higher in London.
- Leasehold flats are rare outside England and Wales; Singapore is the main comparable leasehold market (GMS).
